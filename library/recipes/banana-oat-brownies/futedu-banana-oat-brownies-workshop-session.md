# FUTEDU Nutrition Workshop — Session Master Document
## Fudgy Banana-Oat Brownies: Recovery Treat, No Refined Sugar

**Location:** Residencia Galileo Galilei — kitchen
**Group:** working in teams of 8 (adjust based on turnout)
**Recipe covered:** *Fudgy Banana-Oat Brownies*

---

## 1. Session Dynamic

- Split into teams of ~8. Each team mixes **one full batch** in a bowl (mash bananas → whisk in wet ingredients → fold in dry ingredients → spread in tray), following the steps together.
- Since oven space is limited, bake in **2 rounds of 2 trays** (or however many trays fit at once) rather than all 4 at the same time — a good moment to run a short activity while the first round bakes and the second waits.
- Let each tray cool completely before cutting — cutting too early makes them fall apart, so this is worth calling out explicitly to the group.
- *Extra activity:* **Label Detective** (~10 min) — see Section 4. Runs naturally during the oven-wait / cooling-down time.

---

## 2. What This Recipe Brings & How It Helps Performance

This recipe reframes a "treat" food rather than introducing a new food group — the goal is showing players they can make something that tastes like a dessert without relying on refined sugar or white flour. Ripe banana and a small amount of honey provide the sweetness, rolled oats replace the flour (adding fiber and slower-release carbs instead of a sugar spike), and the peanut butter plus egg add protein and healthy fat on top — turning what's usually an empty-calorie snack into something that can reasonably sit in a recovery window.

**Framing for the group:** not a pre- or post-training staple like the other recipes — more of an "if you're going to have something sweet, this is how to make it work harder for you" option. Good moment to talk about the difference between *ultra-processed* and *homemade* treats, and why the ingredient list matters more than the "dessert" label.

**Talking point:** ask the group to name a store-bought brownie or cookie and compare its ingredient list to this one — makes the refined-sugar/processed-flour point concrete instead of abstract.

## 3. Shopping List — ~33 people, 1 square per person (4 teams, 1 tray each)

Base recipe (1 batch = 1 tray = 9 squares) is scaled **×4** below, one tray per team.

| Ingredient | Per batch (×1) | Total (×4 teams) | Buy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ripe bananas, mashed | 2 medium (~240 g) | 8 medium (~960 g) | ~8–9 bananas |
| Peanut butter (or almond butter) | ½ cup (~130 g) | 2 cups (~520 g) | 2 jars (~350–400 g each) |
| Cocoa powder | ⅓ cup (~30 g) | 1⅓ cups (~120 g) | 1 bag (~150 g) |
| Rolled oats | 1 cup (~90 g) | 4 cups (~360 g) | 1 bag (500 g) |
| Egg | 1 large | 4 large | half a dozen (spares included) |
| Honey | 2 tbsp (~42 g) | ½ cup (~170 g) | 1 jar |
| Baking soda | ½ tsp | 2 tsp | pantry staple |
| Salt | 1 pinch | 4 pinches | pantry staple |

**Combined totals to buy:** ~9 bananas · 2 jars peanut butter · ~120 g cocoa powder · 500 g rolled oats · 4 eggs (+ spares) · 1 jar honey · baking soda & salt (pantry).

**Supplies:** 1 large mixing bowl and whisk per team (4), 4 small baking trays (~20×20cm) lined with parchment paper, oven access for 2 baking rounds, a knife/spatula for cutting once cooled, small containers or bags for take-home portions.

**Allergy note:** peanut butter and egg are both in play here — have an almond-butter swap ready, and this recipe isn't suitable for an egg allergy without testing a substitute (e.g. mashed banana or a flax egg) in advance.

## 4. Activity — Label Detective (~15-20 min)

**Goal:** get teams to actually read and compare a real nutrition label against their homemade brownie, so "processed vs. homemade" becomes something they can point to on paper — not just a talking point from Section 2.

**Materials to prepare beforehand:**
- Printed copies of **`futedu-brownies-label-detective.pdf`** — **one per pair of teams** (2 teams share a sheet), not one each. It has a commercial brownie/snack-bar nutrition label side by side with their homemade brownie's numbers, plus space to write findings — half the printing, same activity.
- Pens/pencils, one per sheet.
- A tray of brownies nearby (so they can double-check the homemade numbers against the real thing, not just trust the sheet).

**How it runs (~10 min):**
1. Hand out the sheet once trays go in the oven (or during cooling) — this is dead time that would otherwise just be waiting around.
2. Each pair of teams compares the two nutrition panels and works through the **3 guided questions** on the sheet (sugar source, ingredient count, "what would you grab before training").
3. Once trays are out and cut, do a **1-2 minute group share**: ask 2-3 pairs to read out just their answer to the last question (what they'd grab before training, and why) — fast, keeps momentum, doesn't need everyone to present everything.

**Facilitator answer key (for your reference — don't hand this out):**

| Question | What to listen for |
|---|---|
| Sugar source | Commercial bar's sugar is mostly added sugar (sugar, glucose syrup) high on the ingredient list; the homemade brownie's sugar mostly comes from banana + a small amount of honey — same "sugars" number on a label can mean very different things. |
| Ingredient count | Commercial bar: 11 ingredients, several unrecognizable additive codes. Homemade: 8 ingredients, all whole foods a player could name. More ingredients isn't automatically bad, but a long list of things you can't picture in your kitchen is worth questioning. |
| Before training | Correct instinct is the homemade brownie *or* neither right before training (fat/protein-heavy, digests slower) — good moment to reconnect back to the Energy Balls session's "fast vs. slow" framing from Session 3. |

**If there's time, one bonus talking point (no need to write it down):** the homemade brownie also has more fiber and protein than the commercial one — oats/banana for fiber, peanut butter/egg for protein — which is the real reason it's more filling, not just "less processed."

**Wrap-up line for the group:** *"The label doesn't lie, but it also doesn't explain itself — reading the numbers is only half the job. Knowing what they mean for you, right before you train, is the other half."*
